# Equipment Data: Viking 1 (Orbiter & Lander)

## 1. Meet the Explorer
- **Equipment Name:** Viking 1 (Viking-B Spacecraft: Orbiter & Lander)
- **Specific Designations:**
  - **Lander Station Name:** Thomas A. Mutch Memorial Station (formally named in January 1982 to honor Thomas A. Mutch, leader of the Viking surface imaging team who died in 1980)
  - **Spacecraft Designations:** Viking-B (re-designated Viking 1 prior to launch when battery issues on Viking-A caused a swap in launch order), COSPAR IDs: 1975-075A (Orbiter), 1975-075C (Lander); NORAD IDs: 08108, 09024
- **Image References:**
  - First photograph ever taken on the surface of Mars (Viking 1 footpad and surface): Credit: NASA
  - First panoramic image of Chryse Planitia taken by Camera 1 on July 23, 1976: Credit: NASA
  - Line drawings of Viking Orbiter and Lander: Credit: NASA
  - Mosaic of Valles Marineris (102 images captured by Viking Orbiter 1): Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech
- **Introduction:**
  NASA's Viking 1 was the first space exploration mission to achieve a fully successful soft landing on Mars, transmit high-resolution color photographs from the Martian surface, and conduct prolonged scientific investigations. Launched on August 20, 1975, Viking 1 consisted of two integrated components: a massive orbital spacecraft based on the Mariner series and a heavily instrumented, nuclear-powered three-legged lander. On July 20, 1976—the seventh anniversary of the Apollo 11 lunar landing—Viking Lander 1 set down safely in Chryse Planitia, opening the modern era of Mars surface science.

## 2. Mission & Purpose
- **Mission Name:** Viking Project (Viking 1 Mission)
- **Program & Management:** NASA Langley Research Center (LaRC - Project Management) / Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL - Orbiter and Telecommunications)
- **Type:** Interplanetary Mars Orbiter & Nuclear-Powered Surface Lander
- **Destination:** Chryse Planitia ("Plains of Gold"), Mars
- **Landing Site Coordinates:**
  - 22.483° N latitude, 47.94° W longitude (nominal / science.nasa.gov; also cataloged in early cartography as 22.27° N, 47.95° W; landed ~17 miles / 28 km from planned target)
- **Touchdown Date Discrepancy Note:**
  - Pre-launch planned landing date: July 4, 1976 (targeted to celebrate the US Bicentennial).
  - Actual touchdown date: July 20, 1976 (delayed by 16 days after orbital photographs revealed the primary site was too hazardous).
- **Purpose & Objectives:**
  - Conduct orbital reconnaissance to map the Martian surface, characterize regional geography, and identify safe landing zones.
  - Deliver a sterilised lander to the surface using an aerodynamic aeroshell, parachute, and terminal descent engines.
  - Search directly for biological activity and organic carbon compounds in the Martian soil.
  - Analyze the inorganic elemental composition of rocks and regolith.
  - Monitor Martian atmospheric composition, surface meteorology (temperature, pressure, wind velocity), and regional seismology over seasonal cycles.

## 3. Meet the Technology
- **Viking Orbiter 1:**
  - **Mass:** Launch mass: 5,132.4 pounds (2,328 kg), of which 3,185.7 lb (1,445 kg) was liquid bipropellant (monomethylhydrazine and nitrogen tetroxide).
  - **Structure:** Octagonal bus 8.2 feet (2.5 m) across and 10.8 feet (3.29 m) tall, equipped with four solar panel wings spanning 34,800 solar cells (producing 620 W at Mars) and two 30-Ah nickel-cadmium batteries.
  - **Propulsion:** Gimballed bipropellant rocket engine generating 1,323 N thrust (delta-V of 1,480 m/s).
  - **Orbiter Science Instruments:**
    1. **Visual Imaging Subsystem (VIS):** Twin redundant slow-scan 475-mm vidicon television framing cameras (1056 x 1182 pixels) providing resolution up to 8 meters from low orbit.
    2. **Mars Atmospheric Water Detector (MAWD):** Infrared grating spectrometer measuring water vapor absorption at 1.4 micrometers.
    3. **Infrared Thermal Mapper (IRTM):** Multi-channel radiometer measuring surface and atmospheric temperatures from -130°C to +57°C.
    4. **Radio Science:** S-band and X-band tracking measuring gravity fields, solar corona plasma, and atmospheric occultation profiles.
- **Viking Lander 1:**
  - **Mass:** Landed dry mass: ~1,448 pounds (657 kg); total separated lander mass before atmospheric entry: 2,156 pounds (978 kg). (Note: A typographical error in early web spec tables listed "2.2 pounds (657 kg)", which was an OCR corruption for landed dry mass).
  - **Structure:** Six-sided welded aluminum structure supported by three extended legs with crushable honeycomb footpads (equilateral triangle base 2.21 m on a side).
  - **Power System:** Two SNAP-19 Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generators (RTGs) containing plutonium-238 mounted on opposite sides of the lander inside wind screens, generating ~70 W of continuous electrical power independent of sunlight or dust storms.
  - **Terminal Descent Propulsion:** 12-nozzle monopropellant hydrazine rocket engine (N2H4, 32 N thrust) to prevent thermal surface disruption and chemical contamination.
  - **Communications:** 20 W S-band transmitter with steerable high-gain dish and low-gain whip antenna for direct Earth communication; 30 W UHF antenna (381 MHz) for orbiter relay.
  - **Lander Science Payload (~91 kg total):**
    1. **Two Facsimile Surface Cameras:** 360-degree cylindrical scanning cameras capable of black-and-white, color, stereoscopic, and infrared imaging.
    2. **Biological Laboratory (Automated Life Detection Suite):** Gas Exchange Experiment (GEX), Pyrolytic Release Experiment (PR), and Labeled Release Experiment (LR).
    3. **Gas Chromatograph Mass Spectrometer (GCMS):** Highly sensitive instrument designed to vaporize soil samples and detect organic molecules down to parts-per-billion sensitivity.
    4. **X-Ray Fluorescence Spectrometer (XRFS):** Measured inorganic elemental chemistry.
    5. **Meteorology Boom:** Deployed sensor assembly measuring atmospheric temperature, ambient pressure, and wind speed/direction.
    6. **Robotic Surface Sampler Arm:** 3-meter retractable boom equipped with an electric scoop, temperature sensor, and magnets.
    7. **Seismometer Subsystem:** Three-axis seismometer mounted on the lander body (which **failed to uncage** properly upon landing, yielding no valid surface seismic data; functioning seismometer operated on Viking 2).

## 4. The Journey
- **Launch:** August 20, 1975, at 21:22:00 UT aboard a Titan IIIE-Centaur rocket from Cape Canaveral Launch Complex 41.
- **Mars Arrival & Orbit Insertion:**
  - After three interplanetary trajectory corrections, entered Mars orbit on June 19, 1976.
  - Initial orbital parameters: 932 x 31,255 miles (1,500 x 50,300 km).
  - Orbiter began photographing the planned landing zone; images revealed terrain far rougher than anticipated, prompting scientists to spend a month surveying alternative sites.
- **Lander Descent & Touchdown (July 20, 1976):**
  - Lander separated from orbiter at 08:32 UT on July 20, 1976.
  - Deployed aeroshell and parachute, fired terminal hydrazine landing thrusters, and touched down safely at 11:53:06 UT in Chryse Planitia.
  - Minutes after landing, Camera 1 initiated scanning and transmitted the first historic photo of the Martian surface.
- **Surface Operations & Soil Testing:**
  - On July 28, 1976, the robotic sampler arm extended, dug into the regolith, and delivered soil to the biological laboratory and GCMS.
  - Conducted prolonged weather monitoring, sending daily (and later weekly) meteorological reports for over six years.
- **Mission Lifespans & Conclusions:**
  - **Orbiter 1:** Maintained operations through extended and continuation missions, completing 1,489 orbits and returning 34,200 high-resolution images. On August 7, 1980, its attitude-control gas was depleted; mission controllers fired its propulsion system to boost its orbit from 357 × 33,943 km to 320 × 56,000 km to prevent impact before at least 2019, before commanding it into shutdown.
  - **Lander 1:** Continued operations for 6 years, 116 days (2,245 sols) as the Viking Monitor Mission. On November 11, 1982, an erroneous software command transmitted from Earth accidentally overwrote the high-gain antenna pointing algorithms, cutting off communications permanently.

## 5. Scientific Discoveries
- **First In-Situ Chemical & Soil Analysis:**
  - Discovered that Martian surface regolith is an iron-rich clay containing abundant silica, iron, sulfur (up to 100 times higher than Earth crustal rocks), and calcium.
- **The Search for Organic Molecules & Life:**
  - The three biology experiments returned perplexing results: the Labeled Release experiment gave a rapid release of radioactive gas (weak positive), but the GCMS detected **no organic compounds** down to parts-per-billion sensitivity.
  - Scientists concluded the activity was caused by non-biological inorganic oxidants (peroxides and superoxides) produced in the soil by intense solar ultraviolet radiation.
- **Meteorology & Global Atmospheric Cycles:**
  - Recorded surface temperatures from -123°F (-86°C) before dawn to -27°F (-33°C) in the afternoon.
  - Observed annual global atmospheric pressure oscillations of ~30%, proving that immense quantities of atmospheric carbon dioxide freeze out and sublimate between the north and south polar ice caps seasonally.
- **Orbital Planetary Mapping:**
  - Orbiter 1 imaged gigantic ancient outflow channels, dry riverbeds, volcanic shields (Tharsis bulge), and detailed morphology across Valles Marineris, confirming that immense volumes of water once carved the Martian crust.

## 6. Where Is It Now?
- **Viking Lander 1 (Thomas Mutch Memorial Station):**
  - Rests intact on the plains of Chryse Planitia at coordinates 22.483° N, 47.94° W. Inactive since communications failed on November 11, 1982.
- **Viking Orbiter 1:**
  - Shut down on August 7, 1980. Left in an orbit raised to 320 × 56,000 km specifically to ensure orbital stability and prevent atmospheric entry and impact prior to 2019, preserving planetary protection. Orbit is subject to long-term secular gravitational and solar radiation perturbations.
- **Why Left Behind:** Designed as one-way planetary observation systems with no recovery or sample-return hardware.

## 7. Its Legacy
- Provided humanity's first direct visual and physical contact with the Martian surface.
- Established the benchmark for planetary protection sterilization standards, heating the entire lander spacecraft to ensure no terrestrial microbes contaminated Mars.
- Transformed scientific understanding of Martian geology, demonstrating that Mars had a dynamic, water-rich past.
- The unresolved questions regarding the Viking biological experiments and soil oxidants directly motivated all subsequent NASA Mars missions, from Mars Pathfinder to the Mars 2020 Perseverance sample collection campaign.
